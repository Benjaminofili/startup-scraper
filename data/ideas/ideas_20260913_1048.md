## 1. RideFare Guard  

**Problem** – Riders repeatedly lose money because the fare shown at booking often spikes mid‑trip, drivers take wrong routes, and the platforms delay withdrawals (Play‑Store complaints #4‑13).  

**Solution** – A lightweight Android app that:  
1. Pulls real‑time price estimates from the public APIs of Uber, Bolt, OPay, and local mini‑cabs (via web‑scraping where APIs are closed).  
2. Shows a *price‑guarantee* range before the ride is accepted and flags any price‑change risk.  
3. Lets users log the final fare, route deviation, and driver behaviour; the data is crowdsourced to produce a “trust score” for each driver.  
4. Generates a pre‑filled dispute email to the platform with the logged evidence (optional).  

**Who pays** –  
*Primary*: Frequent commuters (students, office workers) in Lagos, Abuja, Port‑Harcourt who take >2 rides/week.  
*Secondary*: Ride‑hailing platforms that want a “trust‑badge” API (future B2B).  

**Price** – ₦1 500 / month for a “Premium” tier (unlimited fare‑history, driver‑score alerts, ad‑free). Free tier limited to 5 rides/month, ads from local merchants.  

**Existing competitors** –  
*Direct*: None that combine price‑prediction + driver‑trust crowdsourcing for Nigeria.  
*Indirect*: Google Maps “price estimate” (very rough), local forums (manual), existing ride‑hailing apps (no external estimator).  

**Monetization fit** – Nigerian commuters are price‑sensitive but willing to pay a small monthly fee for predictable fares (e.g., many already pay ₦500‑₦1 000 for data‑bundles). However, conversion from free to paid is historically low (<5 %).  

**First week (launch steps)**  
1. **Data‑scrape** public fare‑estimate endpoints of Uber/Bolt (use the free AWS Lambda tier).  
2. **Build MVP** Android UI (list of providers, price range, “log ride” button).  
3. **Run a 2‑day pilot** with 30 university students; collect feedback and refine driver‑score algorithm.  

**Why now** – The surge of complaints on the Play Store (price hikes, driver ignorance) shows a pain point that is growing as ride‑hailing expands into secondary cities.

## 2. AdShield NG  

**Problem** – Users abandon apps as soon as a pop‑up ad appears; the Play Store reviews list “I always exit the app whenever a pop‑up ad appears” (review #1).  

**Solution** – An Android VPN‑based ad‑blocking app that:  
* Intercepts HTTP/HTTPS traffic locally (no root needed).  
* Blocks known ad‑networks and “pop‑up” domains using an open‑source filter list (e.g., EasyList).  
* Allows a whitelist for “essential” apps (banking, payment) to avoid breakage.  
* Shows a simple “Ad‑blocked” badge in the notification bar.  

**Who pays** – Mobile users who use free apps but hate intrusive ads; especially students and young professionals (15‑30 y) who spend >2 hrs/day on free apps.  

**Price** – Free tier with mandatory “donate” prompt; Premium ad‑free experience at ₦500 / month (removes donation prompt, adds premium filter updates).  

**Existing competitors** –  
*AdGuard* (blocked on Play Store, paid).  
*Blokada* (open‑source, not on Play Store, requires manual sideload).  
*Built‑in “Lite” modes* of some apps (partial).  

**Monetization fit** – Users are accustomed to free ad‑blocking tools; willingness to pay is low (<3 %). The premium tier is more of a “donation” than a hard sell.  

**First week**  
1. Fork the open‑source **Blokada** codebase, replace branding.  
2. Publish a **beta** on a private Google Group (invite 20 friends).  
3. Collect crash logs & ad‑block efficacy data; iterate UI for “whitelist” simplicity.  

**Why now** – The Play Store complaints are fresh, and recent Google policy changes have made it harder for ad‑blockers to stay on the store, creating a brief window for a compliant, low‑risk product.  

**Feasibility scores**  

| Metric | Score |
|--------|-------|
| Investment | **9** (open‑source code, free AWS for analytics) |
| Passive Income | **4** (revenue relies on low‑price subscriptions) |
| Team Size | **9** (1 Android dev can handle core, 1 marketer for ASO) |
| Time to Market | **8** (fork & publish in <1 week) |

| Monetization Fit | **3** (price‑sensitive, expects free) |
| Regulatory Risk | **8** (no illegal content, just traffic filtering) |

## 3. AutoReply‑Subject  

**Problem** – Automated email replies often strip the original subject, making follow‑ups confusing (GitHub issue “Display Email Subject also for Automated Reply”).  

**Solution** – A lightweight Gmail add‑on (web‑based backend + Chrome extension) that:  
* Detects when a user sets an out‑of‑office or canned reply.  
* Prepends “Re: [Original Subject]” to the auto‑generated message body and subject line.  
* Offers a simple UI in Gmail Settings to toggle on/off per filter.  

**Who pays** – Small‑business owners, freelancers, and NGOs in Nigeria that rely on Gmail for client communication (≈ 10 k‑30 k users).  

**Price** – ₦1 000 / month per mailbox (or ₦8 000 / year). Free tier limited to 2 auto‑reply rules.  

**Existing competitors** –  
*Gmail’s native “Vacation responder” (does not preserve subject).  
*Third‑party tools like **Boomerang** (paid, but broader feature set).  
*Zapier/IFTTT integrations (complex, not UI‑friendly).  

**Monetization fit** – Small businesses in Nigeria do spend on SaaS (e.g., Paystack, Zoho) when the ROI is clear. A ₦1 000/month tool that reduces mis‑communication can be justified.  

**First week**  
1. Register the Gmail API project (free tier).  
2. Build a simple Node.js webhook on AWS Lambda to rewrite outgoing auto‑reply messages.  
3. Publish the Chrome extension and run a 48‑hour beta with the university’s student‑run NGOs.  

**Why now** – The GitHub issue is fresh, and many Nigerian NGOs are moving to remote work, increasing reliance on email auto‑responses.  

**Feasibility scores**  

| Metric | Score |
|--------|-------|
| Investment | **8** (Gmail API free, Lambda free tier) |
| Passive Income | **6** (subscription can be fully automated) |
| Team Size | **7** (1 backend dev, 1 Android/web dev for Chrome extension) |
| Time to Market | **7** (API work + UI ~2 weeks) |
| Competition | **5** (Boomerang exists but is generic; niche is subject‑preservation) |

## 4. OSM Nigeria  

**Problem** – New contributors to OpenStreetMap are confused (“Make your first edit to OpenStreetMap”) and lack a localized onboarding experience.  

**Solution** – An Android app that:  
* Guides users through the first edit with step‑by‑step map‑capture (using the phone camera + GPS).  
* Shows a list of “high‑impact” mapping tasks in Nigerian cities (e.g., missing roads, schools).  
* Awards “badge points” redeemable for small vouchers (data bundles, coffee) supplied by local sponsors.  
* Syncs automatically with OSM via the public API.  

**Who pays** – NGOs and humanitarian groups (e.g., Red Cross, Missing Maps) that need accurate maps; they can sponsor the voucher pool. Also, local telecoms may sponsor data‑bundle rewards for brand exposure.  

**Price** – Free for end‑users. NGOs pay a **subscription of ₦20 000 / month** for a branded “campaign” page and access to analytics on contributions.  

**Existing competitors** –  
*OSM’s own web “Learn” portal (global, not mobile‑first).  
*Mapillary (photo‑based mapping, but not OSM‑focused).  
*Local “Mapathon” events (offline).  

**Monetization fit** – NGOs have budgets for mapping projects and often pay for tools that increase volunteer productivity. The SaaS‑style subscription is realistic.  

**First week**  
1. Scaffold the Android app with the **osmdroid** library (open‑source).  
2. Pull a list of “unmapped” OSM elements for Lagos via Overpass API.  
3. Reach out to the **Missing Maps** chapter in Nigeria for a pilot partnership (they’ll provide the voucher budget).  

**Why now** – Humanitarian mapping is accelerating after recent floods and conflict‑related displacement in Nigeria; donors are looking for scalable digital tools.  

**Feasibility scores**

|--------|-------|
| Investment | **9** (all libraries open‑source, AWS free for analytics) |
| Passive Income | **5** (NGO subscriptions, but need ongoing relationship) |

*AI Weekly* (global, English, ad‑supported).

*Local tech newsletters* (sporadic, not AI‑specific).
